A Room Built in the Hallway

On tires, care, and the firewall between them.

BY IMRAN HAFIZ · AUSTIN, TX

In 2017 two ads came out that I still think about. Pepsi made one with Kendall Jenner. I wrote the other one, and a van in the Bronx is still running because of it.

You remember the Pepsi ad. A protest for no particular cause, a line of riot police, and Kendall Jenner walking off a photo shoot to hand a cop a can of Pepsi. The crowd cheers. Pepsi pulled it within two days and apologized.

Nobody remembers the other one, because it was small and it worked. It was a short film for Michelin called Inroads to the Community. I was a junior creative at VIRTUE, VICE's in-house agency. My creative director, Tyler Pierce, let juniors run with ideas when the ideas were good, and he didn't flinch when an idea meant telling a client something they didn't want to hear.

[ THE_BRIEF ]

Michelin's brief was one sentence:

Show how tires = care.

A tire grips a road. Care is how one person looks after another. That equals sign was asking a lot: make people feel our tires are a form of love, and make it something we can put on a reel. Every brand runs some version of this brief. Oil equals stewardship. Fast fashion equals self-expression. The algorithm equals connection. Everyone in the room knows the equals sign is a stretch, and the job is to make it look good enough that nobody has to say so.

I call that condition the Pretend Era. Nobody in it is a villain. Everyone knows the game is broken, but whoever says so first pays the whole cost of saying it, so the meetings go on and the briefs keep coming. Pepsi happens, and then another Pepsi.

I walked into that meeting knowing something Michelin had stopped noticing about itself. Most people under thirty-five have no idea the Michelin Guide, the red book and the stars, comes from the tire company. The Michelin brothers started it in 1900 to give French drivers a reason to drive farther and wear out their tires. It may be the best piece of content marketing ever made, and a century later most of the people who care about it don't know it has anything to do with rubber.

Michelin knew this the way you know your own middle name. Nobody in the room was twenty-four or lived in Brooklyn, so nobody had asked whether a twenty-four-year-old in Brooklyn made the connection. I said I didn't think young people knew the guide was theirs. The room went quiet. It wasn't a hostile quiet; it was people hearing something they had half-known for a long time.

The brief on paper stayed the same. The real question in the room became how Michelin could matter again to the people it wanted to reach. I didn't think another equals sign would get there. Michelin had to show up somewhere it had no obvious right to be, and earn its place by being useful.

[ THE_CHEF ]

I talked to a few chefs and landed on Carlo Mirarchi, who runs Roberta's in Bushwick and Blanca, the two-Michelin-star tasting room behind the pizza ovens. He said yes before I finished explaining. Other chefs gave me a careful yes. Carlo said yes like he'd been waiting for someone to call, and I've learned to trust that kind of yes more than almost anything else in creative work.

The idea was to bring Michelin-starred chefs to the Grand Central Food Program, the largest mobile soup kitchen in the world. Its vans run out of the Coalition for the Homeless and have served hot meals along the same New York routes, every night, for decades. I didn't want a gala or a fundraiser. I wanted the chefs cooking the real food for the real vans on a normal night, and a film about the vans and the people eating, with Michelin present the way a good host is present at dinner.

When I pitched it, the senior client from Michelin North America said the line I've thought about most since:

“We don't feel we have the right to speak in this space.”

He wasn't being defensive. He was being careful. Michelin had come to VICE to become culturally relevant, and the same caution had them fighting this idea for three months. They didn't want to look like they were renting moral weight they hadn't paid for.

I told him you earn the right to speak in a space by showing up and being useful. Massimo Bottura cooks for refugees and the unhoused through Food for Soul. José Andrés has fed more people in disaster zones than most governments. Nobody thinks their stars got smaller. He thought about it and said okay. It was a cautious okay, the kind that gives you enough rope to hang yourself or to show him something he hadn't seen. I think that's the most honest okay a senior client can give a junior creative.

[ THE_NIGHT ]

On the day of the shoot, Carlo came in early and stood next to the GCFP's regular kitchen crew while they cooked. They're committed people who have done hard work for a long time, and most of them aren't trained chefs. He tasted things and suggested a little more salt here, a different finish there. The food that went out that night was theirs, a little better for having a two-star chef beside them for a few hours. I've come to think that's what expertise is for.

The vans ran their usual routes, stopped where they always stop, and handed plates to the people already waiting. Nothing was staged. We joined the work.

The senior client came too. He didn't have to be in Harlem at nine at night for a shoot he'd doubted for months, but he was there, in the kind of expensive outdoor jacket corporate men wear to show they're ready for weather. He stood to the side and watched food go out the back of a van. At some point he turned to me and said:

“I didn't understand why this might work until today. Now I get it.”

Three months of meetings, references and arguments hadn't gotten him there. Standing on a sidewalk watching a hungry person get dinner did. It's the most important thing I learned in my twenties about how institutions change: you can't explain your way to it. You stand next to someone while it happens to them. The meeting is for permission. The site is for understanding. After that night, he stopped fighting the project.

[ THE_TIRES ]

The film aired and did fine, the way films like that do. If the story ended there, I wouldn't be writing this.

The part I'm proudest of is something nobody asked for. I wrote into the project that Michelin would put new tires on the GCFP's vans. Tires are expensive, the program runs on thin margins, and Michelin makes tires. So in this one case, tires really did equal care. The rubber kept the vans on the road that carried the food, and the brand's product and the value it claimed were connected in a way you could touch.

A few months later the GCFP's director asked me to go back to Michelin for something bigger: a whole new van, so the program could open a route in the Bronx. I wrote the ask. My argument was that a van would still be feeding people years after any ad bought with the same money had been forgotten. Michelin said yes.

I checked with the director this year. The van is still in service. (The tires were a one-time donation.) The project didn't win a Cannes Lion or anything else, and if you search for it you won't find much. But a van that exists because of a meeting I sat in as a junior creative fed someone tonight, and that's the only measure of creative work that has ever made sense to me.

[ THE_HALLWAY ]

The thing I was slow to understand was myself.

For most of my twenties I kept hearing that I was too much of something for whatever room I was in. Too activist for journalism, too journalist for brand work, too theoretical for the people shipping on Friday, too operational for the people writing theory. I'd studied public policy at Duke. At VICE I was an intern doing associate-producer work on the Emmy-winning HBO team, then got invited onto the pre-launch VICE News crew. Then I spent most of a decade at VIRTUE as a story researcher, junior creative, strategist and insights publisher.

The people who told me I didn't fit weren't wrong about me. They were wrong about the categories. They assumed journalism, brand work and media theory belonged in separate rooms, and by that logic I fit none of them. I was too busy trying to squeeze into one room to see that the walls between them were the broken part.

In 2017 the hallway was VIRTUE: the place where journalism instincts met brand budgets and media theory, and, quietly, the part of the company paying the rent. The Michelin project could only have happened there. A newsroom would have refused the sponsor, a brand team would have refused the rigor, and a theory department would have refused the deadline. The hallway only asked whether the thing worked.

Then 2020 happened. Watching the institutions in charge of public communication try to reach people in a crisis, I saw that a junior brand strategist at VIRTUE, paid a fraction of what a senior FEMA communications officer earns, had a more accurate model of how information moves than the institutions whose job it was. The FEMA officer was probably smarter and more experienced. But they'd been trained to write the right message and send it through official channels, a model that had stopped working about fifteen years earlier, and nobody inside could say so without questioning the premise that kept everyone employed.

That's when I stopped trying to squeeze into rooms. I built The Auxiliary, named for the engine that helps the main engine run when it can't run alone. It existed to do on purpose what the Michelin project did by accident: work in the space between disciplines and produce what none of them could produce on their own. Aux Labs carries that forward.

Aux Labs is a room built in the hallway.

[ THE_RULE ]

Timur Kuran named the underlying dynamic in 1995: preference falsification, people saying in public what they don't believe in private. My claim is that it has spread from politics into nearly every institution I've worked in. Seen that way, most of the last decade's public failures look less like incompetence and more like an equilibrium nobody wanted and nobody could leave alone.

Exhortation won't get us out; exhortation is how we got here. Mechanisms can: rules that change the payoffs, so the thing everyone privately wants becomes the thing it's rational to do in public. I've been building one, a ten-step sequence for how trust gets built and spent that I call the Trust-Repair Cascade. The step that matters here is where brands, sponsors and private capital come in.

The old institutions aren't coming back the way they were, the new ones aren't built yet, and much of the money to build them sits with companies trained by twenty-five years of CSR to spend it on the appearance of care. It's in their long-term interest that things keep working. Rory Sutherland has written well about the short-termism that stops them acting on that interest. I'm interested in the mechanism that would let them act without spoiling what they're trying to help.

CSR's answer is for the brand to commission, shape and approve the work, and the result rings hollow because of how it was commissioned. Pepsi in 2017 was that answer. Gillette in 2019 was well-meant and rejected on arrival. Bud Light in 2023 managed to fail in both directions at once, punished by one audience for a gesture that did nothing for the other, because the gesture wasn't connected to anything that could help anyone.

Here's the rule I took from the Michelin project and have spent nine years trying to formalize:

Brands can't commission public-interest work. They can only amplify work that was independently conceived.

The research, the listening and the artistic idea have to happen where the sponsor can't touch them, paid for by foundations, universities and public-health bodies whose job is exactly that. The sponsor comes in later, at amplification. It doesn't buy editorial control. It buys proximity to legitimacy it could never have made itself, because making it required the sponsor's absence.

That sounds like a restriction, but it sends each kind of money where it works best. Foundation money is good at diagnosis and rigor. Brand money is good at scale and distribution. The firewall between them makes sure that when the brand arrives, the work is still worth amplifying.

Michelin was an accidental proof. The GCFP had existed for decades, and Michelin couldn't have invented it. What Michelin did was show up late, after the work was already good, and help it go further: tires, a van, and a film that pointed at the work without claiming it. The brand didn't commission care. It amplified care that was already happening, and earned the association it had wanted all along.

So here is my question, if you run a brand, sit on a foundation board, or sign checks meant to make things a little better: what if the only thing your money could do was help good work travel further than it could on its own? Would that feel like losing control, or like relief?

A van in the Bronx has been running for nine years because someone said yes to that question without knowing they'd been asked it.

I still think about his face.
