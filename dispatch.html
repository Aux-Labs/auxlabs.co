[ DISPATCH_001 ] 14 SEP 2026 ~5 MIN

Dario Amodei is describing a traffic jam.

There is a cheap measurement that would tell us whether a slower frontier is actually possible. Nobody has taken it.

c/o IMRAN HAFIZ · AUSTIN, TX

Dario Amodei published an essay this month called We Must Pace the Frontier. Not stop — pace. “We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models,” he writes. “Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain.”

The reasoning is a gap: “fully addressing the risks requires even more prudence — not just investing in risk prevention, but pacing the rate of capabilities advancement so that risk prevention has time to keep up.”

He proposes three steps, and they widen as they go: Embedded Evaluators, Democratic Coordination, Global Coordination. Inside the lab, then among allies, then everyone. A full pause he mentions and sets aside — he supports floating it, and thinks it unlikely, “defecting from such an agreement by evading monitoring could radically shift the balance of global power.”

I want to make one narrow claim about that ladder, and then tell you about a highway.

The claim is this: those aren’t three separate ideas. They’re one problem, sorted by how much force the solution requires. And there is a forty-thousand-dollar way to find out how much force is actually needed — which nobody has spent, because the question sits in the gap between the people who study incentives and the people who build things.

[ THE_MERGE ]

I live in Austin. If you have ever crawled up I-35 through the middle of this city, you have personally sat inside the mechanism I’m about to describe.

When a highway lane closes, the fastest thing everyone can do is use both lanes all the way to the cone and take turns at the merge point. Engineers call it the zipper merge and they have measured it: field data from Minnesota shows backups roughly forty percent shorter. One Michigan work zone went from six miles of congestion to three. This is not a folk remedy. It’s settled traffic engineering.

Almost nobody does it.

Here is why, and it’s the part that matters. Most drivers privately suspect the zipper is correct. But they believe everyone else thinks late merging is cheating. So they get over a mile early to be polite. And a few drivers appoint themselves enforcers, straddling the open lane to block the “cheaters.” That blocking is what makes late merging genuinely dangerous — which then proves to everyone that late mergers are reckless.

The people punishing the behavior are manufacturing the evidence for punishing it.

Nobody in that traffic jam wants to be in it. Every individual is behaving reasonably given what they believe about everyone else. The belief is wrong, and the wrongness is invisible, because the only way to find out what other drivers actually think is to ask them — and nobody has.

[ NOW_READ_THE_LADDER_AGAIN ]

Every lab may privately prefer a slower frontier. Each one believes its competitors won’t slow. So nobody slows.

And the ones who move fastest cite the others moving fast as the reason they must. The race is the evidence for the race.

If that sounds familiar, it should. The structure is identical: a private preference that most people may hold, a belief about everyone else that contradicts it, and behavior that generates the proof of its own necessity.

Amodei’s first step, Embedded Evaluators, is not primarily a technical control. It is a device for making beliefs visible. It puts someone in the room who can say what is actually happening, to everyone, at once. In traffic that’s the sign reading USE BOTH LANES TO MERGE POINT, and where they’ve installed those, the jam gets shorter.

[ THE_FORK ]

Two different problems. They look identical from outside.

This is the fork, and it decides which rung of his ladder is load-bearing.

Problem one: everyone would rather slow down, and everyone is stuck because they think nobody else would. That’s a belief problem. You fix it by measuring what people actually think and showing them the result. Evaluators, shared standards, published commitments — these work, because the only thing holding the bad equilibrium in place was a false belief about the room.

Problem two: slowing down genuinely costs you, and whoever slows first genuinely loses. That’s not a belief problem. Information doesn’t touch it. You need something with teeth — enforceable agreements, verification, consequences.

From outside, these look the same. Everyone races in both. But they need completely different interventions, and an intervention designed for one is close to useless against the other. Spend three years building trust-and-transparency mechanisms against a problem that was really the second kind and you will have done excellent work on the wrong thing.

Here is what makes this worth writing down rather than merely noticing. Amodei’s three steps ladder from the first kind of solution to the second. Embedded Evaluators makes beliefs visible — that is an answer to problem one. Global Coordination builds enforcement — that is an answer to problem two. And when he explains why a full pause is unlikely, his reason is defection: someone evading monitoring could shift the balance of power. That is a problem-two reason.

So the ladder is hedged across both diagnoses, which is a reasonable thing to do when you don’t know which you face. But it means the first rung and the last are answers to different questions, and the cost of guessing wrong runs in both directions: build evaluators against a defection problem and you have built a window into a room that was never going to cooperate; build enforcement against a belief problem and you have spent years of political capital on a treaty that a survey and a published result could have replaced.

Nobody has measured which one this is.

[ WHAT_IT_WOULD_COST_TO_FIND_OUT ]

In traffic, we know how to measure it. You ask drivers two questions: what do you think the right thing to do is, and what do you think other drivers think the right thing to do is. The gap between those answers is the whole mechanism.

Our published prediction on the highway version is that the gap exceeds fifteen points. If it comes in under fifteen, the mechanism is wrong, and we publish the null result with the same prominence we’d have published a win.

The same instrument works on labs. Not on drivers — on the people who actually set release timelines, under conditions where the answer costs them nothing. What would you prefer? What do you believe your counterparts prefer? Nobody has run it. Not because it’s hard, but because it sits in the seam between economics, which designs incentives and rarely builds, and safety research, which builds and rarely asks this particular question.

Forty thousand dollars bought the highway version. The lab version is cheaper, because the population is smaller and easier to reach.

[ WHERE_THIS_BREAKS ]

I should say what I’m not claiming, because a consultant telling you that a traffic study explains AI policy is a genre, and not a trustworthy one.

Drivers are individuals. Labs are organizations, and an organization’s “private preference” is a fiction that resolves into a fight between a safety team and a board. Whatever the survey measures, it isn’t one mind.

Traffic is also a clean assurance problem: if everyone zippers, everyone is better off, full stop. I do not know that AI capability racing has that shape. It may genuinely be the second problem — the one where slowing first is a real loss, and no amount of shared information changes that. If it is, then the mechanism I’m describing is the wrong tool, and the honest answer is that his third rung matters far more than his first.

That’s precisely why the measurement is worth taking. I’m not claiming to know the answer. I’m pointing out that the answer is cheap, decision-relevant, and unmeasured — and that we are three years into building solutions without having established which problem we’re solving.

[ THE_PREDICTION ]

Stated in advance, so you can hold me to it.

If the gap between private preference and perceived peer preference among frontier-lab decision-makers exceeds fifteen points, this is an assurance problem, and Embedded Evaluators is the rung that does the work.

If the gap is under fifteen points, it is not, and everything short of Global Coordination with real verification is theater. I’ll publish that result as prominently as the other one.

Either someone funds the measurement, or the silence becomes its own finding about how this field allocates attention. We learn something in both cases, which is how I prefer to design things.
